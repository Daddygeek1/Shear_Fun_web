What does "Endangered" really mean?

Imagine your favorite stuffed animal was the only one left in the whole world. If something happened to it, it would be gone forever. That's basically what endangered means for a wild species — there are so few left that one bad event (like losing their home) could wipe them out completely.

🔍 Fun fact: Scientists use a scale called the IUCN Red List to track how close a species is to disappearing. "Critically Endangered" is the highest danger level — even scarier than "Endangered"!

In the Most Danger

Federally Endangered in Missouri
🐚

Paddle Snail

Plagiopora plicifera
Freshwater Mussel
Endangered
🧬 What is it?
A tiny freshwater mussel — think of it like a living rock with a shell! It filters water and helps keep rivers clean, kind of like a natural water purifier.
📍 Where does it live?
Only in a handful of Missouri streams. Fewer than 5 known spots! That's why it's ranked "critically imperilable" in our state.
⚠ Why is it in trouble?
Damaged riverbeds, pollution, and dams blocking its path make it hard for these little mussels to find food and make babies.
💡 Cool fact!
Paddle Snails can live for over 100 years! But because they grow so slowly, a single bad year can wipe out a whole population.
🐟

Hine's Banded Darter

Hypseleotris hinesii
Freshwater Fish
Endangered
🧬 What is it?
A super small fish — only about 4 inches long — with a cool dark band across its body. It looks a bit like a tiny, streamlined torpedo.
📍 Where does it live?
Rocky-bottom streams in parts of Missouri, Oklahoma, and Texas. In Missouri, there are very few rivers where you'd still find one.
⚠ Why is it in trouble?
Silt (muddy sediment) covers the rocks it needs to live on, and channelizing (straightening) streams destroys its home.
💡 Cool fact!
Despite being tiny, this fish is a super swimmer! It can navigate fast-flowing water better than most fish its size. It was named after biologist J.C. Hine.

Proposed for Endangered status

Fender's Blue Butterfly

Leptotes fenderi
Butterfly
Proposed E
🧬 What is it?
A small, pretty blue butterfly. The males shimmer with a deep blue; the females look more brownish. They're about the size of your thumbnail.
📍 Where did it live?
Missouri used to be home to Fender's Blues, but they've disappeared from the state. They still exist in a few other places, which is why the feds are about to protect them.
⚠ Why did it disappear?
Its larva (caterpillar) stage only eats one specific type of milkweed. When that plant is lost, the butterfly can't survive.
💡 Cool fact!
Fender's Blues were named after Aaron Fender, a Texas rancher, where they were first found. Scientists are now trying to bring them back to places they used to live!

Prairie Fringed Orchid

Platanthera integrifolia
Wildflower
Wait List
🌿 What is it?
A beautiful white orchid with fringed petals, like a tiny lily of the valley. It grows on open prairies and blooms in early summer.
📍 Where did it grow?
Missouri prairies — but it's now extremely rare here. Only a handful of states still have healthy populations.
⚠ Why is it rare?
Most Missouri prairies have been turned into farmland. This orchid also needs a very specific type of fungus to grow, so it's a double whammy.
💡 Cool fact!
Its seeds are so tiny and tiny that a single flower can make thousands of them — but most will never grow into a plant. It takes 3–5 years from seed to first bloom!
🐝

Henslow's Bumble Bee

Bombus henslowii
Bumble Bee
Wait List
🍯 What is it?
A chunky, fuzzy bumble bee with a black body and a rusty-orange band across its middle. About the size of a grape.
📍 Where was it seen?
Once found in Missouri prairies and meadows, now it's a ghost species here — we only have old records.
⚠ Why is it in trouble?
Pesticides, loss of wildflower meadows, and even parasitic mites from Europe are making life very hard for native bumble bees.
💡 Cool fact!
Henslow's Bumble Bee can hum at a specific frequency to vibrate flowers and release pollen — a trick called "buzz pollination." It's like the bee has its own built-in speaker! 🔊
🦋

Karner Blue Butterfly

Lycaeides melissa samuelis
Butterfly
Wait List
🧬 What is it?
A small, silvery-blue butterfly. The males are bright blue on top; the females are greenish-white underneath with a blue band. Very pretty up close!
📍 Where was it found?
Historically in Missouri's oak savannas and pine barrens. Now extremely rare or gone from the state.
⚠ Why is it in trouble?
Its caterpillars only eat wild blueberries and cranberries. Clearing forests for development removes both the berries and the oak trees it needs for shade.
💡 Cool fact!
Karner Blues were first described from Blue Hill, Massachusetts. They're one of the most "hard to tell apart" butterflies — scientists used to think they were just a color variation of another species!
🦋

Pygmy Rusty-Tipped Butterfly

Eresia hecale
Butterfly
Wait List
🧬 What is it?
A tiny, striking butterfly with black and orange wings and rust-colored "tips" — hence the name. It's one of the smallest butterflies in North America.
📍 Where was it found?
Once in the sandy, pine-wooded areas of south Missouri. Now considered extirpated (locally extinct) in the state.
⚠ Why is it in trouble?
It needs sandy, open pine habitats with specific host plants (mainly in the Celastrus family). Development and forest succession (trees growing too dense) destroy that habitat.
💡 Cool fact!
"Pygmy" comes from Pygmy midgets in Greek myth — tiny people! This butterfly is truly miniature: only about 1.5 inches from wingtip to wingtip.
🦋

White-Patched Fivestain

Pseudolycaena damo
Butterfly
Wait List
🧬 What is it?
A small copper-brown butterfly with five orange spots and a white patch on the underside of its hindwing. "Fivestain" = five stains!
📍 Where was it found?
Dry, sandy uplands in parts of Missouri and the Southeast. Now very rare or absent from the state.
⚠ Why is it in trouble?
It needs open, sandy habitats with specific legume host plants. When those areas are paved, farmed, or allowed to turn into dense forest, the Fivestain disappears.
💡 Cool fact!
The "five stains" on its wings are actually eye-spots — tiny markings that look like little eyes to scare off predators like birds. Evolution's trickster! 👀

🌱 What Can You Do?

Even kids can be conservation heroes. Here are real ways to help these endangered friends:

🌻
Plant Native Flowers
Milkweed, wildflowers, and berry bushes feed butterflies and bees.
🚫
Skip the Pesticides
One spray can kill hundreds of bumble bees and butterflies at once.
🥾
Stay on the Trail
In streams and prairies, stepping off-path can crush eggs, mussels, or rare plants.
📸
Spot & Report
See something weird in a creek or meadow? Take a photo and tell a grown-up or call the MDC.
📖
Learn & Share
The more people know, the more they care. Tell your friends what you learned today!
🌊
Protect Water
Mussels and darters need clean, flowing water. Pick up trash near streams. Every bit counts.
